# 16 — Jazz

Jazz grew out of blues, ragtime and brass-band music in New Orleans in the early 1900s and
became the most harmonically sophisticated popular music of the 20th century. For a composer
its essentials are: **swing feel**, **seventh-chord harmony with extensions**, the
**ii–V–I**, standard **forms**, and a **rhythm section** whose players improvise their parts
within strict roles.

## 16.1 Swing feel

- Eighth notes are played **long–short** (chapter 03). The ratio depends on tempo: ballads and
  medium tempos swing hard (≈ 0.62–0.67), up-tempo tunes (250+ BPM) become nearly straight
  (≈ 0.55).
- **Beats 2 and 4** are accented (hi-hat foot, finger snaps), the opposite of the march-like
  1 and 3.
- Phrases often start or end on **off-beats**, and many accents are **anticipations** (a chord
  arriving on the "& of 4" of the previous bar).
- Time feel matters more than notes: the bassist plays slightly **on top of** the beat and the
  drummer's ride cymbal defines the pulse.

Typical tempos: ballad 50–80 BPM, medium swing 120–180, up-tempo/bebop 200–320.

## 16.2 Harmony: sevenths are the default

In jazz the **seventh chord** is the basic unit; triads sound plain. Extensions (9, 11, 13)
and alterations (♭9, ♯9, ♯11, ♭13) add colour (chapter 07).

| Function | Typical chords (in C) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tonic | Cmaj7, C6, C69, Cmaj9, Cmaj7♯11 | maj7 or 6 instead of a triad |
| Subdominant | Dm7, Dm9, Fmaj7, Fm6 | ii7 is the usual pre-dominant |
| Dominant | G7, G9, G13, G7♭9, G7♯9, G7alt, G7sus4 | dominants carry the tension, so they get the most alteration |
| Diminished | B°7, C♯°7 | passing chords between diatonic chords |

### The ii–V–I

The engine of jazz harmony: **ii7 – V7 – Imaj7** (Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7), roots falling in fifths
(chapter 05). Most standards are chains of ii–Vs into different keys.

- **Minor ii–V–i**: iiø7 – V7♭9 – im6 (Dm7♭5 – G7♭9 – Cm6). The ♭9 comes from the
  harmonic minor scale.
- **Guide tones**: the 3rd and 7th of each chord define its quality. Dm7 has F (3rd) and
  C (7th); G7 has B (3rd) and F (7th); Cmaj7 has E (3rd) and B (7th). Through the ii–V–I
  the 7th of each chord falls a half step to the 3rd of the next (C → B, then F → E), while
  the 3rd holds and becomes the next chord's 7th (F stays F, then B stays B). A melody or
  voicing built on guide tones sounds "inside" the changes without playing roots.

### Substitutions and turnarounds

| Device | Example in C | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Tritone substitution** | Dm7 – **D♭7** – Cmaj7 | G7 and D♭7 share the tritone B–F; bass now moves chromatically |
| **Secondary dominants** | Cmaj7 – **A7** – Dm7 – G7 | V of ii |
| **Backdoor ii–V** | **Fm7 – B♭7** – Cmaj7 | ♭VII7 resolves up a whole step; softer than V7 |
| **Turnaround** | Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7 | I–vi–ii–V at the end of a form, leading back to the top |
| **iii–VI–ii–V** | Em7 – A7 – Dm7 – G7 | substitute for I–vi–ii–V |
| **Diminished passing chord** | Fmaj7 – **F♯°7** – C/G | chromatic bass line between IV and I |
| **Coltrane / modal** | long static modal vamps (Dm7 for 16 bars) | the opposite of ii–V density |

### Chord–scale relationships

An improviser (and a melody generator) chooses notes from a scale that fits each chord:

| Chord | Scale | Characteristic / "avoid" note |
|---|---|---|
| maj7 | Ionian, or **Lydian** (♯4) | the natural 4 clashes with the 3rd: avoid holding it |
| m7 (ii) | **Dorian** | natural 6 |
| 7 (V) | **Mixolydian** | natural 4 is a passing note only |
| 7♯11 | Lydian dominant | ♯4 |
| 7alt / 7♯9♭13 | **altered** (7th mode of melodic minor) | ♭9, ♯9, ♯11, ♭13 |
| 7♭9 | half–whole diminished | ♭9, ♯9, 13 |
| m7♭5 | Locrian (or Locrian ♮2) | ♭5 |
| °7 | whole–half diminished | |

### Voicings

| Voicing | Notes | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| **Shell** | root + 3rd + 7th (or root + 7th + 3rd) | guitar, piano left hand, big-band bass section |
| **Rootless "A"** | 3 – 5 – 7 – 9 | piano (the bassist has the root) |
| **Rootless "B"** | 7 – 9 – 3 – 5 | piano; alternate A/B to voice-lead through ii–Vs |
| **Drop 2** | close voicing with the 2nd voice from the top dropped an octave | guitar, horn sections |
| **Quartal** | stacked 4ths (E–A–D–G) | modal jazz ("So What" voicings) |

With rootless voicings a ii–V–I in F becomes A–B♭–D–F → G–B♭–D–E → G–A–C–E: three
4-note chords with only stepwise motion.

## 16.3 The rhythm section

### Drums

- **Ride cymbal**: "ding, ding-a ding, ding-a" — beats 1, 2, &2, 3, 4, &4 with the off-beats
  swung. It is the time-keeper.
- **Hi-hat foot** closes on 2 and 4.
- **Bass drum** "feathered" softly on all four beats (barely audible), plus accents ("bombs").
- **Snare** comps: sparse, conversational accents and ghost notes that answer the soloist.
- **Brushes** for ballads; **trading fours**: soloists and drummer alternate 4-bar solos.

### Bass

- **Two-feel**: half notes (root, fifth) — often for the first chorus or the head.
- **Walking**: four quarter notes per bar. Rules of thumb:
  1. Beat 1 of each chord: the **root** (or another strong chord tone).
  2. Beats 2–3: chord tones and scale steps moving toward the next chord.
  3. The last note before a change: an **approach note** a half step above/below the next
     root (chromatic), or its fifth (dominant approach).
  4. Mostly stepwise; leaps are recovered by step; stay in E1–C3.
- **Pedal points** under intros, vamps and endings.

### Piano and guitar comping

- Short, syncopated chord stabs rather than continuous accompaniment.
- **Charleston rhythm**: hits on beat 1 and the "& of 2" — the classic swing comp figure.
- **Anticipations**: chord on the "& of 4" tied into the next bar.
- **Freddie Green style** (Count Basie): guitar plays short quarter-note shell voicings on every
  beat, almost a percussion instrument.
- Comping leaves space for the soloist; piano and guitar avoid playing at the same time.

## 16.4 Forms and arrangement

| Form | Layout | Examples (forms, not melodies) |
|---|---|---|
| **32-bar AABA** | A (8) A (8) B (8, "bridge") A (8) | "I Got Rhythm" (rhythm changes), many standards |
| **32-bar ABAC** | A B A C | "All of Me" type |
| **12-bar blues** | jazz blues changes (chapter 17) | "Billie's Bounce", "Blue Monk" |
| **Modal** | long vamps on one chord/mode | "So What" (AABA, D Dorian / E♭ Dorian) |
| **Rhythm changes** | AABA in B♭: I–VI–ii–V turnarounds in A; III7–VI7–II7–V7 in B | bebop's favourite vehicle |

A performance usually goes: **intro** (often the last 4–8 bars of the form, or a vamp) →
**head** (the melody, once or twice) → **solos** (each soloist takes one or more choruses of
the form) → **trading fours** → **head out** → **ending** (tag the last phrase 2–3 times, a
ritardando, or a vamp).

Big bands (saxes, trumpets, trombones, rhythm) add **backgrounds** behind soloists,
**soli** passages (a section in harmony) and a climactic **shout chorus**.

## 16.5 Substyles

| Style | Era | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Early / New Orleans | 1910s–20s | collective improvisation, 2-beat feel, tuba/banjo |
| Swing / big band | 1930s–40s | 4/4 walking, riffs, sections, dance tempos |
| Bebop | 1940s | fast tempos, dense ii–Vs, chromatic lines, small combos |
| Cool / West Coast | 1950s | lighter sound, counterpoint, relaxed tempos |
| Hard bop | 1950s–60s | blues and gospel roots, strong grooves |
| Modal | late 1950s– | static harmony, scales over chords, quartal voicings |
| Bossa nova | late 1950s– | Brazilian, **straight 8ths**, 2-bar clave-like patterns, soft dynamics |
| Jazz waltz | | 3/4 with swing; ride "1 2& 3", bass in dotted halves or walking in 3 |
| Fusion / jazz-rock | 1970s– | rock/funk rhythms, electric instruments, odd meters |
