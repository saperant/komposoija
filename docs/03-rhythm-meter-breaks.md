# 03 — Rhythm, Meter and Breaks

Rhythm is arguably the most important element of pop and rock. A great groove with simple
harmony works; complex harmony with a weak groove usually doesn't.

## 3.1 Beat, tempo and meter

- **Beat (pulse)** — the steady pulse you tap your foot to.
- **Tempo** — speed of the beat in **BPM**.
- **Meter** — how beats are grouped into **bars (measures)**, with a pattern of strong and
  weak beats.

### Common tempo ranges

| BPM | Feel | Typical styles |
|---|---|---|
| 60–80 | Slow | Ballads, slow soul, power ballads |
| 80–100 | Mid-slow | Hip-hop, R&B, reggae (often felt half-time) |
| 100–120 | Mid | Pop, funk, classic rock, disco (~110–125) |
| 120–130 | Mid-fast | Dance-pop, house (~120–128), pop-rock |
| 130–160 | Fast | Rock, pop-punk, indie |
| 160–200+ | Very fast | Punk, hardcore, thrash, drum & bass (~170–175) |

### Time signatures

A time signature has two numbers: **top = beats per bar**, **bottom = note value of a beat**
(4 = quarter, 8 = eighth).

| Signature | Grouping | Feel / use |
|---|---|---|
| **4/4** ("common time") | 1 2 3 4 — strong on 1, secondary on 3 | ~95 % of pop/rock |
| 3/4 | 1 2 3 | Waltz, some ballads ("Norwegian Wood", "Manic Depression") |
| 6/8 | 1-2-3 4-5-6 (two dotted-quarter beats) | Slow rock ballads, doo-wop, "We Are the Champions", "Nothing Else Matters" |
| 12/8 | four groups of three | Slow blues, soul ballads ("Red House", "At Last") |
| 2/4 | 1 2 | Marches, polka, some punk |
| 5/4, 7/8 etc. | Odd meters | Prog rock ("Money" is 7/4), math rock |

**Simple meter** — beats divide into two (2/4, 3/4, 4/4).
**Compound meter** — beats divide into three (6/8, 9/8, 12/8).

## 3.2 Subdivision

Counting 4/4 at increasing resolution:

```
Quarters:   1       2       3       4
Eighths:    1   &   2   &   3   &   4   &
Sixteenths: 1 e & a 2 e & a 3 e & a 4 e & a
Triplets:   1 trip let 2 trip let 3 trip let 4 trip let
```

The density of subdivisions in a part (especially hi-hat and rhythm guitar) strongly
determines perceived energy: eighth-note hi-hats feel steady; sixteenths feel busy/driving.

## 3.3 Accent, backbeat and downbeat

- **Downbeat** — beat 1 of the bar; also used loosely for "on the beat".
- **Upbeat / offbeat** — the "&"s between beats.
- **Backbeat** — accent on **beats 2 and 4**. *The* defining rhythmic feature of rock, pop,
  R&B and most popular music since the 1950s, usually played by the snare drum (or claps).
- **Four-on-the-floor** — kick drum on every quarter note (disco, house, dance-pop).

## 3.4 Syncopation

**Syncopation** = emphasis on a weak beat or offbeat, or a note that *anticipates* a strong
beat. It creates groove and forward motion.

- **Anticipation / push** — a chord or note arrives an eighth (or sixteenth) **before** the
  bar line and is tied over. Hugely common in pop and rock: the chord change on the "& of 4".
- **Offbeat chords** — reggae/ska "skank" on the & of each beat.
- **Clave and tresillo** — the 3+3+2 pattern (`x..x..x.` in eighths) underlies countless pop,
  reggaeton and dancehall rhythms.

```
Tresillo (8 eighths):   x . . x . . x .
Straight eighths:       x . x . x . x .
Backbeat (quarters):    . X . X
```

## 3.5 Swing and shuffle

- **Straight** feel — eighths are equal.
- **Swing / shuffle** — the first eighth of each pair is longer, the second shorter, roughly
  a 2:1 triplet ratio (long-short). Blues, early rock'n'roll, boogie, jazz.
- **Swing amount** in DAWs is a continuum: 50 % = straight, ~66 % = triplet swing.
  Subtle 16th swing (~54–58 %) is the secret of a lot of hip-hop and funk grooves.
- **Half-time feel** — snare on beat 3 only, so the music feels half as fast though tempo is
  unchanged. Used for heavy choruses, breakdowns, trap.
- **Double-time feel** — snare on every "&" or on 2 and 4 at double speed; feels twice as fast.

## 3.6 Rests

A **rest** is a notated silence of a given duration (whole rest, half rest, quarter rest …).
Silence is part of rhythm: space between notes is what makes a riff or a groove breathe.
Many famous riffs are defined as much by their rests as their notes.

## 3.7 Breaks, stops, fills and breakdowns

"Break" is used in several related senses in pop/rock; all are about **interrupting** the
established groove to create interest or mark a structural boundary.

| Term | What it is | Typical length | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fill** | The drummer (or another player) breaks from the groove to play a short flourish, usually at the end of a phrase | ½–1 bar (sometimes 2 beats) | Signals "a new section is coming" |
| **Stop / stop-time** | Band hits a chord on beat 1 (or a pattern of hits) then goes silent, leaving space for vocal or solo | 1–4 bars, often repeated | Drama, focus on vocal ("Blue Suede Shoes", "Black Dog") |
| **Break (solo break)** | The band drops out leaving one instrument (often drums) playing alone | 1–8 bars | Spotlight, surprise |
| **Drum break / breakbeat** | A section where only drums (and maybe percussion) play | 2–8 bars | Origin of hip-hop sampling ("Amen break", "Funky Drummer") |
| **Breakdown** | A section with stripped-back arrangement, often lower energy, before building back up. In metalcore: a heavy half-time, syncopated chug section | 4–16 bars | Contrast, rebuild energy, crowd moment |
| **Hits / kicks / stabs** | Whole band accents the same rhythm together | Few beats | Punctuation, tightness |
| **Pickup / anacrusis** | Notes before the first downbeat of a phrase | < 1 bar | Leads into the phrase |
| **Turnaround** | A short chord pattern at the end of a section that leads back to the start | 1–2 bars | Loops the form (blues: I–IV–I–V) |
| **Tag** | Repetition of the last line/phrase at the end | 2–8 bars | Ending |
| **Dropout / "drop"** | Instruments (often kick and bass) suddenly removed, then return | 1–2 beats to 1 bar | Builds anticipation for the next downbeat |

### Fill conventions

- Fills usually occur in the **last bar** (or last beats) of a 4- or 8-bar phrase.
- Longer phrase boundaries get bigger fills: end of 4 bars → small fill (2 beats);
  end of 8/16 bars or end of a section → full-bar fill.
- The fill ends and a **crash cymbal + kick** lands on beat 1 of the new section.
- Tom fills typically descend from high tom → low tom → floor tom.

## 3.8 Phrasing and hypermeter

Bars group into **phrases**, almost always in powers of two in pop/rock: **2, 4, 8, 16 bars**.
This "hypermeter" is how listeners feel where they are in the song. A phrase of 4 bars
often has an internal shape of *statement – answer – statement – variation/cadence*.

Odd phrase lengths (e.g. a 6-bar or 9-bar section, or a 2/4 bar inserted) are used for
surprise — the Beatles did this often — but should be deliberate exceptions.

## For the pipeline

- Use a **grid**: bars × beats × subdivisions (e.g. 16 steps per 4/4 bar for drums,
  or ticks at 480 PPQ for precise timing).
- Store the **meter** and **feel** (`straight`, `swing(ratio)`, `half_time`) per section.
  In komposoija: `song.section(..., meter="7/8")`, plus `tempo=` for a sudden change and
  `tempo_to=` for a ritardando or accelerando (`docs/song-dsl.md`).
- Apply swing as a post-process: delay every off-step by `(ratio − 0.5) × step_length × 2`.
- Encode phrase boundaries explicitly so the drum generator knows where fills go and where
  the crash lands.
- Breaks/stops are **arrangement events**: a section-level mask over instruments
  (e.g. `stop: bars 7–8, all except vocal`).

## Exercises

1. Count and clap: straight eighths, then the same with swing.
2. Clap a backbeat (2 & 4) while tapping quarter notes with your foot.
3. Listen to "Back in Black" (AC/DC) and identify the stops and fills.
